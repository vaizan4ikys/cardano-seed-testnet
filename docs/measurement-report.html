Cardano Testnet Measurements

A private Cardano network with three block-producing pools runs inside the SEED emulator. Four virtual machines on one Mac stood in for a small distributed system. We measured how fast new nodes join, how fast blocks and transactions travel, how much sensor data the chain can carry, and what a foreign network does to it. This page says why each measurement was taken and what the numbers mean.

What this means for CyberTel

Read this first. All four machines are virtual machines on one Mac, so they share CPU and disk. Delay, loss and speed limits are emulated on the sending side of each node. The chain is private and small, with three pools, and many results come from one to three runs. Read the numbers as sizes of effects and as comparisons between settings, not as predictions for a real wide-area network.

What we would do with it

These are our recommendations, drawn from the numbers. They are judgements, not measurements.

Batch the readings. Send 100 or more readings per transaction.
Keep headroom. Stay under about 70% of the limit, about 200 readings per second on the testnet.
Keep the pools close. Within about 100 ms of each other with 5-second blocks.
Keep sites close to a pool. Connect gateways directly to a pool, or one relay away at most; every further hop adds about 1 s of waiting.
Synchronise the clocks. Every site needs time synchronisation with an error well under 2 s.
Help slow sites join. Offer a database snapshot or a nearby relay for sites on slow lines.
Plan for a split pool. Alarm when a pool's tip differs from the others for more than a few minutes, and keep the wipe-and-resync routine ready.

1 · How long does a new node need to join?

Question
How does the time to join grow with the size of the chain, with link delay, and with packet loss?
Why
A new site, or a node that crashed and lost its data, must catch up before it can serve anyone. This sets the recovery time.
How it was measured

A fresh node with an empty database starts, dials a pool, and downloads the chain. The timer stops when its newest block is less than 20 s old. A transaction load kept the blocks about 91% full while the chain grew for 6 hours (12 checkpoints, one every 30 min).

Without extra delay the join takes 3.5 s at 27 MB and 12.4 s at 239 MB; with 100 ms it takes 13 s and 81 s, so delay costs more as the chain grows. Copy speed levels off near 20 MB/s (cause untested, probably the VM's CPU or disk). Three nodes joining together needed about 1.35 times as long as one (16.7 s against 12.4 s at the last checkpoint); two checkpoints were 2.6 and 3.3 times slower, reason unknown.

Delay and chain size: the numbers

On the large chain each millisecond of delay costs about 1.3 s of join time at hop 1 and 2.4 s at hop 2 (256 s and 480 s at 200 ms). The doubling happens once, from hop 1 to hop 2, and does not grow with deeper hops (section 3). On the small chain a millisecond cost 0.02 s: delay punishes big chains because the download needs many round trips.

Packet loss

One percent loss barely matters at 0 ms (18.6 s against 16.4 s). At 100 ms plus 1% loss hop 1 took 121 s, but hop 2 did not finish within 900 s in either repetition. TCP slows sharply when loss and delay meet on a link that carries bulk data; this is expected and was not tested further.

● Solid · trend is clean over 12 checkpoints; the 3-node values are noisy (n = 2)
A node that joins over a slow or lossy link needs minutes, not seconds. For distant sites a database snapshot is probably a better start than a full download.

2 · How fast does a block travel?

Question
When a pool makes a block, how many milliseconds later does a node one or two hops away have it?
Why
Slow travel means pools build on old blocks and create forks. It also bounds how fresh a reading can be at a remote site.
How it was measured

Every node writes a millisecond timestamp when a block becomes its newest. The first node to log a block is the forger. The others' delay is their timestamp minus that one. The VM clocks were aligned to about 1 ms and measured again for every run.

With almost empty blocks hop 1 sees a block after about 1 times the delay and hop 2 after about 4 times (100 ms gives 107 ms and 412 ms). With full blocks it is about 3 times at hop 1 (323 ms at 100 ms, 608 ms at 200 ms) and about 10 times at hop 2. A 60 KB block does not fit into one TCP window, so it needs several round trips (our explanation, not isolated in a test). The mainnet-like chain, also with nearly empty blocks, gave the same factors (209/812 ms at 200 ms, 512/2021 ms at 500 ms), which points to block size, not block rhythm.

Which link adds what?

Delay was put on one joiner at a time, with full blocks, 8 minutes per setting (about 100 blocks each).

Which link adds what: the numbers

A delay on a node's own link costs that node about 3.1 to 3.3 times the delay (322 ms at 100 ms, 617 ms at 200 ms). A delay on hop 1 adds about 4 times the delay more for hop 2. Delay on hop 2 does not hurt hop 1 (17 ms). With both links at 100 ms hop 2 gets blocks after 1034 ms: the effects add up.

● Solid · windows fixed and re-analysed; hop-1 and hop-2 results agree between runs
In a chain of relays, delay accumulates over every hop and a full block pays three to four times each delay. Pools should sit close to each other, and sensor gateways should dial a pool directly.

3 · Many hops: does the cost per hop stay the same?

Question
In a chain of up to 6 nodes, each one connected to the previous, how do block arrival, join time and transaction time grow with the number of hops?
Why
Section 2 measured only 1 and 2 hops, so it could not say whether the cost per hop stays the same. Sensor sites will often reach the chain through relays.
How it was measured

Six nodes in a chain on the three joiner VMs (two nodes per VM). Node k dials node k-1, and node 1 dials a pool. Every node has the delay on what it sends, so beyond the first link the delay acts in both directions of a link. Delays of 0, 50 and 100 ms, full blocks, 7 minutes of samples per delay. The chain had grown to about 90 to 100 MB.

The lines are straight. The first hop costs about 3.2 times the delay and every further hop about 7.1 times: at 100 ms, 316 ms for hop 1, then 711 ms more per hop, which gives 3,871 ms at hop 6 (316 + 5 × 711); at 50 ms the steps are 360 ms. With no added delay each hop adds about 6 ms. A relay adds a request and a download, each carrying the delay.

Join time against depth: details

Join time does not grow with depth: a node talks only to the node it joins from, so only that link counts. Hop 1 needs about half as long because the pool's side of that link has no added delay. Each value is the median of three repetitions on a chain of 627 MB; they agree within 3% (one hop-3 run at 100 ms took 397 s against 380 and 386 s), with no outlier in 54 joins. An earlier run on a 100 MB chain showed the same pattern (32 s at hop 1, about 61 s from hop 2 on), plus one 35 s join at hop 5, probably a reconnection pause.

The join-time formula

The formula from section 1 fits when the size is the data actually received: 2 s + MB × (0.042 + 0.0029 × delay in ms). For 627 MB it gives 28 s at no delay (measured 28 s), and from hop 2 on, where the delay part is doubled, 392 s at 100 ms (measured 383 s) and 210 s at 50 ms (measured 194 s).

How much data a join receives

The nodes received 79 MB at block 1,491, 100 MB at block 1,834 and 627 MB at block 10,700, about 53 to 59 KB per block: a chain of 10,700 blocks is about 0.6 GB.

Transactions through many hops

A transaction needs about 3 times the delay per hop to reach the pool's waiting room: 0.04 s at no delay, and at hop 6 about 0.9 s (50 ms) and 1.8 s (100 ms). The time until the block is too noisy to quote.

● Solid · straight lines for 3 delays and 6 hops; the join times are the median of 3 repetitions
Every relay between a site and the pool adds about 7 times the link delay to block arrival. A site should be as few hops from a pool as possible. Joining a new node costs the same at any depth.

4 · How long until a transaction is in a block?

Question
How long does a transaction take to reach the pool's waiting room, and how long until it is in a block?
Why
This is the delay between a sensor reading and the moment it is stored.
How it was measured

A node submits one metadata transaction. We time (a) the moment the pool's mempool holds it and (b) the moment the node sees it in a block. Six to ten transactions per setting. The transaction is built and signed on the pool machine, because the funded key is there, and only submitted at the node on the VM. So the building and signing time, and the link from a sensor to its node, are not in these times.

5 · When do pools start to fork?

Question
How many blocks are lost to forks as the delay between the pools grows?
Why
Each orphaned block is wasted work and shows that pools disagree for a moment. If the pools were in different cities, this tells us how far apart they may be.
How it was measured

Delay was added to all three pools' own traffic. For each delay we counted for 20 minutes (testnet) or 30 minutes (mainnet-like), with the transaction load running: blocks forged by the pools against blocks that ended up on the chain. The difference is the orphans.

On the testnet 6% of the blocks are lost even without extra delay, because two pools are sometimes elected in the same second. Up to 100 ms nothing changes; at 300 ms 22% are lost and at 600 ms 27%, and the chain slows from 11.6 to 8.5 blocks per minute. The mainnet-like chain tolerates more: no orphans at 0 ms, 6% at 500 ms, 13% at 1000 ms, with the block rate down to 90% and 68%.

● Solid for the testnet and for 0–1000 ms on the mainnet-like chain ✕ Do not use · mainnet-like, 2000 ms: pool 1 lost its connections to the others and forged alone for about 80 minutes (see Trust)
With 5-second blocks, pools should stay below about 100 ms from each other. With 20-second blocks, 500 ms is acceptable. Many city pairs inside Europe are less than 50 ms apart.

6 · How many readings per second can the chain take?

Question
Where is the limit, and how does waiting time behave when we get close to it?
Why
It tells how many sensors one network can serve and how much batching is needed. At 100 readings/s the chain serves about 6,000 sensors that report once a minute.
How it was measured

From inside pool 1, transactions of 1, 10, 100 or 300 readings were submitted at a fixed rate for 2 minutes per step. Each transaction was timed from its planned send time to the block that holds it. The load was stopped during this test.

The limit is about 300 readings/s, as calculated: five 300-reading transactions per block, about twelve blocks per minute, 307 readings/s. Batches of 1 and 10 readings reached only 20 and 148 readings/s because the test program (one cardano-cli process per transaction) could not send faster; the node itself could take about 47 small transactions per second. No transaction was lost or rejected: when the node's waiting room (129 KB) is full, the submit command waits instead of failing. Waiting time near the limit: the numbers

Below the limit a reading waits about 3 to 5 s (median). Above it the waiting time explodes: with 100-reading transactions the median is 4.7 s at 200 readings/s, 30 s at 400 and 103 s at 600. Even far below the limit some readings wait long, because blocks come at random moments: p90 is 17 s with single readings at 2 per second.

The mainnet-like chain, with 3 blocks per minute of 88 KB, stored about 120 readings/s, against 330 on the testnet. The calculation gives about 105, so the numbers fit. Each step saw only about 6 blocks, so single values are noisy.

Seen from many gateways, each with its own wallet

How the gateway test was run

A sensor system sends from many sites, so we checked that the limit does not depend on where the transactions come from. 6 (later 12) gateway nodes ran on three VMs. Each made its own key inside its container, and one transaction from the funded genesis key paid every gateway 100 ADA. Then all gateways signed their own transactions and submitted them at the same second through their own node; a transaction is timed from its planned send time to the block that holds it, as seen at the gateway's node. Fresh testnet, load off, every gateway one hop from a pool (relays follow below). Steps lasted 5 minutes, and 15 for the overload test, because a 2-minute step has only about 25 blocks and varies by about ±20%.

The two curves are almost the same. Up to about 250 readings per second everything offered is stored, with a typical wait of 5 to 9 s; at 300 the wait jumps. A comfortable load is about 200 readings per second: typical wait about 7 s, slowest 1% about 32 s. With 12 gateways, 400 offered gave 290 stored per second.

8 · Finality, outages and failures

Question
How long until a stored reading can no longer be undone? What happens when a node or a pool is cut off, and does the chain heal?
Why
A system that stores evidence needs to know when a record is final, and how it behaves when an organisation's pool loses its connection.
How it was measured

5-second testnet with the load running. Finality: 12 small transactions, each timed until the chain tip is 60 blocks deeper (60 is the security parameter k). Catch-up: a node is cut off (all packets it sends dropped), reconnected, and timed until it is within 20 seconds of the chain clock. Failures: pools stopped or cut off by stopping their network router, then switched back on. We time how long until the three pools show the same tip.

Finality

A transaction is in a block after about 6 s (6.1 s on average). It is final, 60 blocks deep, after about 310 s, a little over 5 minutes (295 to 332 s over 12 transactions): the 60 blocks need about 305 s at about 12 blocks per minute.

ExampleA sensor reading is submitted at 12:00:00. It is in a block at about 12:00:06 and anyone can read it. From 12:05:10 on, it can no longer be undone by a fork.

ExampleAn organisation's pool loses its connection for 3 minutes. The other two pools continue, and 66 s after the link is back the pool agrees with them again. After a loss of 15 minutes its operator must wipe the pool's database and resync, which takes under a minute.

The chain survives the loss of one pool, and even two, and heals within about two minutes after a short cut. A pool that stays isolated for about 15 minutes needs a manual resync.

9 · Traffic, many nodes and wrong clocks

Question
How much data does a node move in normal operation? What does it cost a pool when many nodes connect to it? What happens when a node's clock is wrong?
Why
A site on a mobile data plan needs the daily volume. A pool that serves many gateways needs to cope. Sensor sites often have clocks that drift.
How it was measured

Traffic: the byte counters of each node's network card over 10 minutes, with the load off (empty blocks) and on (full blocks). Many nodes: 1, 4, 8 and 12 nodes dial pool 1 directly, 5 minutes each. Clock: one node gets its clock set wrong by 1 to 10 s, joins, and is watched for 2 minutes.

ExampleA site that reads the chain on a 10 GB monthly data plan would use about 30 GB per month at full capacity, which does not fit. At a tenth of the capacity it uses about 3 GB, which does.

Many nodes on one pool

The pool's CPU use stayed at 3.3 to 3.6% from 1 to 12 nodes. What grows is its upload: from 154 MB per hour with one node to 569 MB per hour with 12, about 45 MB per hour for every node.

A wrong clock

The clock test: details

A node accepts a block only if its time is no more than 2 s in the future of the node's clock. A clock that is ahead causes no problem (in all three runs it behaved like a correct clock). A clock behind by 2 s or more makes the node drop the connection 6 to 13 times in the 2 minutes; it reconnects and catches up, so it is up to date for part of the time: median of three runs 86% at 2 s behind, 95% at 3 s, 68% at 5 s and 50% at 10 s. The runs differ a lot (41% to 100% at 10 s behind), so only the direction is firm: the further behind, the worse. The first sync took about 29 s. The first attempt did not restore the clock between the offsets, so its errors added up (seen in the node's logs); we repeated it twice with a proper restore, and the logged header ages now match the offsets (worst case 2.5, 5.5 and 10.0 s). One run (2 s behind, third repetition) overlapped a 15-minute sleep of the Mac and lacks its first sync time.

▲ Rough · the traffic and many-nodes numbers are solid; the clock results come from three runs per offset and vary between runs
A pool serves a dozen nodes without trouble. A sensor node with a clock that runs behind by more than 2 seconds is unreliable, so every site needs working time synchronisation.

10 · How did the machines cope?

Question
How much CPU, memory and disk do the machines that host the nodes use, and did they limit our results?
Why
This is the point of the four-VM case: what a site needs to host a node, and whether the VMs on one Mac distorted the measurements.
How it was measured

A sampler read the CPU use and memory of the cardano-node processes and the free memory of every VM once a minute during four runs (growth, the large-chain matrix, the fork test and the outsider test). In steady state a node is light: a pool node uses about 145 MB and a few percent of one core, and a following node about 200 MB. The one heavy moment is the sync of a new node. Disk, and how the Mac itself coped

Disk: seedvm used 27 of its 96 GB after about 12,000 blocks. A joining node's database was about 1.2 GB at 10,700 blocks, twice the 625 MB it received, because it also builds its own ledger state. At full load the chain grows about 1 GB per day.

The Mac: in the spot checks the VMware processes held 0.15 to 1.4 GB of memory each (1.4 GB for the pool VM). About 47% of the Mac's memory was free, swap use was 157 MB of 1 GB, there was no thermal or performance warning, and the load average was about 1. We did not record the Mac's CPU during the runs, so how much the VMs competed for it is known only from one case: a fibre join took 97 s instead of 47 s while other work ran on the Mac.

ExampleA small gateway VM with 2 CPUs and 2 GB can host a node. It uses about 200 MB of memory and a few percent of a core, and up to 1.3 cores for the minute it needs to join.

Hosting a node is cheap. A 2 GB VM with 2 CPUs is enough, and the CPU only matters during a join. The pools never came near the limits of their VM.

11 · What does an outsider network do to ours?

Question
If a node from a different Cardano network connects to one of our pools, who refuses it, how fast, and does the real chain notice?
Why
A sensor system needs to know whether a foreign or hostile node can disturb it. Two things can differ between networks: the genesis (the start of the chain) and the magic number.
How it was measured

On a spare VM a rogue node with its own genesis, its own pool and its own keys forged its own chain and dialled pool 1 for 10 minutes. We measured 5 minutes before, 10 minutes with the rogue and 5 minutes after. Two cases were run: the same magic (42), and a different magic (43).

The magic number is only a filter, not a password. A rogue with the right number connects, and every 12 seconds it tries again. What stops it is the chain itself: its first block does not match the real genesis. The pools never pulled the rogue's chain, because they only follow the peers in their own topology file. The first two attempts of the same-magic case failed with a clock-skew error. That was an artifact of the rogue's start-up timing, not the real rejection reason.

An outsider cannot make the pools adopt a foreign chain and cannot slow the real one. It can keep dialling. Pool 1 should only be reachable from the sites that need it.

How far to trust the numbers

Several problems appeared during the measurements. All were found and either fixed or marked. Those that touch numbers you may quote:

Mainnet-like fork test, 2000 ms. Pool 1 was cut off, so the row is invalid and marked "do not use".
Propagation windows. For part of the large-chain matrix the window used the VM clock instead of the Mac clock. Fixed; the data was re-analysed from the saved logs.
51 lost transactions. In one 5-minute gateway step five gateways each had 10 or 11 transactions refused for 60 seconds in a row and gave up, all at the same moment, about 3 minutes into the step. The step was repeated four times, and on 2 November in three more full runs (nine more steps, refusal messages now kept): nothing was lost or refused. The cause is unknown.
Failure tests. The block rate "before" each test ranged from 5 to 20 blocks per minute and is not reliable; this page quotes only heal times and rates during the outage.

Limits that apply to everything: all machines are VMs on one Mac, so CPU and disk are shared. Delay and loss are emulated on the sender side only. The chain is small and the pools are few. The "synced" test (newest block less than 20 s old) adds noise of up to 20 s when blocks come every 20 s, so the mainnet-like join times are only a baseline.

What we think but did not prove

The numbers are measured. These explanations of them are not.

Why a full block costs 3 times the delay. Probably several TCP round trips for a 60 KB block. It fits the data (nearly empty blocks about 1 times the delay, full blocks about 3 times), but we never captured the packets.
Why block arrival depends on block size, not rhythm. The mainnet-like chain, with nearly empty blocks, behaved like the testnet with empty blocks. Block size was never changed alone.
Why joins stop speeding up near 20 MB/s. The joining node used up to 1.3 of its 2 cores, so the CPU is the likely limit. Not tested.
Why hop 2 did not finish at 100 ms and 1% loss. We expect TCP to slow down on a lossy, delayed link that carries bulk data. Not tested apart.
Why catch-up after an outage varies from 10 s to 143 s. Checked: nothing is received during the wait, so it is an idle pause. What ends it (we think the node's retry delay) is not confirmed.
Why three nodes joining together were 2.6 and 3.3 times slower in two checkpoints. Unknown.
What refused the transactions of five gateways at once. Unknown (see Trust).
Where healing stops. A cut of 6 minutes heals and one of 15 minutes does not. The limit in between, expected near 60 blocks of the isolated pool's own fork, was not tested.

What is still open

A new block producer. Register a fourth pool on the running chain, give it stake, and measure the time to its first block and the effect on forks. It takes about two epochs (100 minutes).
A producer at the far end of a relay chain. The realistic setup; the per-hop rule of section 3 should apply to its blocks.
Smart-contract cost. A transaction with a validator that checks sensor identities, against plain metadata: size, fee and capacity. Needs a validator tool installed.
Real separate networks. Every result here comes from VMs on one Mac. Machines in different places would show how much of the delay, loss and CPU sharing was an artefact. A packet capture of one block transfer would also confirm the round-trip explanation of section 2.
Failures in between. Cuts of 8 to 12 minutes, to find where healing stops, and repeats of the catch-up times.
